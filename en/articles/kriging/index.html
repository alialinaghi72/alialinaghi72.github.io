What is kriging? A practical guide for resource estimation

Kriging is the most widely used method for estimating grades in block models. This guide explains what kriging actually does, where its weights come from, and what makes a kriged estimate defensible — or misleading.

Key takeaways

  • Kriging is a weighted average of samples, but the weights come from the variogram model, not from distance alone.
  • Unbiasedness plus minimum error variance gives the kriging system, which returns a kriging variance alongside the grade.
  • Kriging automatically declusters grouped samples and screens samples that sit behind closer ones.
  • Kriged estimates are smoother than reality; recoverable resources at the selective mining unit scale need change-of-support methods or simulation.
  • Estimate quality depends on domaining, top-cutting, the variogram model and the search neighbourhood, and must be validated with swath plots and global statistics.

Kriging in plain language

Suppose we want to estimate the copper grade of a 25 × 25 × 10 m block with a few drillholes around it. The simplest approach is to average the nearby samples. Inverse distance weighting goes one step further and gives closer samples more weight. Kriging completes the idea with two important differences: the weights come from a variogram model that describes the actual grade continuity of that deposit, and the geometry of the samples relative to each other is taken into account.

That is why kriging is called the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE): linear because the estimate is a linear combination of samples, unbiased because on average it has no systematic error, and best because it minimises the estimation error variance. Georges Matheron formalised the method in the 1960s, building on Danie Krige's work in the Witwatersrand gold mines, and named it after Krige.

The ordinary kriging equations

In ordinary kriging, the grade of a point or block is estimated as:

Z*(x₀) = Σ λᵢ · Z(xᵢ)   ,   Σ λᵢ = 1

The constraint that the weights sum to one guarantees unbiasedness without knowing the deposit mean, which makes ordinary kriging suitable where the local mean changes from place to place. Minimising the error variance under this constraint, with a Lagrange multiplier μ, gives the kriging system:

Σⱼ λⱼ γ(xᵢ, xⱼ) + μ = γ(xᵢ, x₀)    i = 1 … n

Here γ(xᵢ, xⱼ) is the variogram between two samples and γ(xᵢ, x₀) the variogram between each sample and the estimation point. The left-hand side measures how redundant the samples are with each other; the right-hand side measures how statistically close each sample is to the target. Solving the system gives the weights and the kriging variance:

σ²ₖ = Σ λᵢ γ(xᵢ, x₀) + μ

In block kriging, γ(xᵢ, x₀) is replaced by the average variogram between the sample and the whole block, and the average variogram within the block is subtracted from the variance. The kriging variance depends only on the sample geometry and the variogram model, not on the grade values, so it measures geometric confidence rather than a true confidence interval on grade.

Weights in practice: declustering and screening

The figure below shows ordinary kriging weights for seven samples around a point. The weights were actually computed with this variogram model, and they show two key behaviours of kriging:

  • Declustering: the three closely spaced samples to the west carry largely redundant information, so kriging gives them together about the same weight as the single sample to the east at the same distance, whereas inverse distance gives the cluster nearly three times as much and lets it dominate.
  • Screening: the sample sitting directly behind the closer northern one adds little new information and receives a very small or, as here, negative weight.

Small negative weights are normal; large negative weights point to a poor search neighbourhood or an overly continuous variogram and can produce unrealistic, even negative, grades.

Types of kriging and when to use them

  • Simple kriging (SK): assumes a known global mean. Rarely used for direct estimation, but it is the engine of sequential Gaussian simulation (SGS).
  • Ordinary kriging (OK): assumes an unknown mean that is constant within the neighbourhood; the standard method for most resource estimates.
  • Indicator kriging (IK): transforms the data into 0/1 indicators at several grade thresholds and estimates the probability of exceeding each one; suited to highly skewed, high-nugget distributions such as vein gold.
  • Cokriging: uses a correlated secondary variable — copper to help estimate molybdenum, or a geophysical attribute — to improve the primary estimate.
  • Kriging with a trend (UK, KED): for a mean with a systematic spatial trend, such as grade decreasing away from the core of a deposit.
  • Lognormal kriging: works on log-transformed data; the back-transform is sensitive to distributional assumptions and should be used with care.

Search neighbourhood and kriging neighbourhood analysis (KNA)

Choosing which samples take part in each block estimate matters as much as the variogram itself. The search ellipse is usually aligned with the variogram anisotropy, and parameters such as minimum and maximum samples, maximum samples per drillhole and octant search are set.

In kriging neighbourhood analysis these parameters are varied for a set of test blocks while monitoring metrics such as the slope of regression (true grade on estimated grade) and kriging efficiency. A slope close to one means little conditional bias. Many practitioners look for values around 0.9 or higher in high-confidence blocks, but this is not a rule of the reporting codes and depends on the deposit and the purpose of the estimate.

Kriging efficiency is KE = (BV − KV) / BV, where BV is the block variance and KV the kriging variance. A negative value means the estimate is no better than simply using the global mean.

Smoothing and the grade–tonnage curve

Because kriging minimises error variance, its estimates are smoother than reality: high grades are underestimated and low grades overestimated. When drill spacing is large relative to the block size, smoothing is severe, and a grade–tonnage curve read directly from the kriged model misstates the tonnage and grade above cut-off.

Common remedies are estimating into blocks sized to the drill spacing rather than smaller, change-of-support methods such as uniform conditioning (UC) and localised uniform conditioning (LUC), and conditional simulation to assess uncertainty. For the economic side of this, see the cut-off grade article.

Kriging or inverse distance?

Inverse distance weighting (IDW) is simple and fast and needs no variogram, but it does not decluster and its distance power is an arbitrary choice. When there is enough data for a reliable variogram, ordinary kriging is the standard choice. In practice IDW and nearest-neighbour (NN) estimates are run alongside kriging so that validation can expose any bias.

Common kriging mistakes

  • Estimating without domains, letting samples cross the boundaries of different mineralised zones (hard versus soft boundaries).
  • Not top-cutting extreme grades in skewed deposits.
  • A variogram with too low a nugget, which gives the estimate false confidence.
  • Ignoring anisotropy, or a search ellipse that is not aligned with the geology.
  • A neighbourhood that is too large and over-smooths, or too small and creates conditional bias.
  • Too many samples from a single drillhole, concentrating weight in one direction.
  • No validation: compare the global mean with declustered nearest-neighbour, check swath plots and review sections visually.

Questions

Which software is used for kriging?

Specialist packages such as Isatis.neo, Leapfrog Edge, Datamine, Surpac and Vulcan run kriging, and libraries such as GSLIB or Python's gstools are used for custom analysis.

Is kriging variance the same as grade uncertainty?

Not entirely. Kriging variance depends only on sample geometry and the variogram model and is insensitive to local grade values. Conditional simulation is the better tool for assessing real uncertainty.

How many drillholes are needed for kriging?

There is no fixed number; what matters is having enough sample pairs to build a reliable variogram in each domain. With little data, inverse distance with conservative classification is usually the more sensible choice.
