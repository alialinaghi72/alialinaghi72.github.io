Data and QA/QC

QA/QC of exploration data

A resource estimate is never better than its data. A QA/QC programme shows that laboratory results are precise, accurate and free of contamination, and it underpins both resource classification and investor confidence.

Key takeaways

  • Quality assurance (QA) is the procedures that prevent errors; quality control (QC) measures quality with control samples.
  • Standards measure accuracy, blanks measure contamination and duplicates measure precision at each preparation stage.
  • A standard result beyond ±3 standard deviations, or two consecutive results beyond ±2 SD on the same side, usually fails the batch.
  • Sending a share of pulps to a second (umpire) laboratory reveals inter-laboratory bias.
  • QA/QC results and corrective actions must be documented in the resource report; assay data quality is a JORC Table 1 criterion.

QA versus QC

Quality assurance is everything done before errors happen: written sampling protocols, chain of custody, a laboratory accredited to ISO/IEC 17025, team training and database management. Quality control measures how well that worked: control samples of known or comparable value are inserted into the sample stream and their results are monitored continuously.

Types of control samples

  • Certified reference material (CRM, "standard"): material with a certified grade and known standard deviation that measures accuracy, the closeness to the true value. Use several standards near the cut-off, average and high grades, with a matrix similar to the deposit.
  • Blank: material without the element of interest that reveals contamination. Coarse blanks go through crushing; pulp blanks reveal only contamination during assay.
  • Duplicates: measure precision (repeatability) at each stage — field or half-core duplicates, coarse crush duplicates and pulp duplicates.
  • Umpire assays: a share of pulps sent to a second laboratory to reveal bias between laboratories.

Insertion rates

The reporting codes set no exact rate, but common industry practice is to insert each of standards, blanks and duplicates at about one in twenty samples (5%), so that control samples total roughly 10–15%, with about 5% of pulps sent for umpire assay. Control samples should be inserted blind so the laboratory cannot distinguish them from routine samples.

The standard control chart

Each standard's results are plotted in time order on a control chart. Common acceptance rules:

  • Within ±2 standard deviations: pass.
  • Between 2 and 3 standard deviations: warning; two consecutive warnings on the same side are treated as a failure.
  • Beyond ±3 standard deviations: failure; samples in the batch around it are usually re-assayed.

Beyond individual results, the trend matters: results drifting away from the certified value or sitting consistently on one side indicate systematic bias even if none falls outside the limits.

Evaluating blanks and duplicates

Blank results are compared with the method detection limit; a common threshold is a few times the detection limit (for example three to five times), and anything higher calls for a contamination check — especially when the blank follows a high-grade sample.

Duplicate pairs are assessed with scatter plots, relative-difference plots and the half absolute relative difference (HARD). Targets commonly cited in the technical literature are that at least 90% of pairs fall within about 10% for pulp duplicates, 20% for coarse duplicates and 30% for field duplicates; for high-nugget gold these targets are usually wider.

When QC fails

A failed control sample does not mean throwing data away; it starts a documented process: check for sample swaps or labelling errors, contact the laboratory, re-assay all or part of the batch, and record the result and decision in the database. The resource report should summarise QA/QC results, failures and corrective actions.

Pre-estimation checklist

  • Every assay batch contains standards, blanks and duplicates, and their results have been reviewed.
  • Failed batches have been re-assayed and replacement results stored in the database.
  • Primary laboratory bias has been checked against umpire results.
  • Historical data and different drilling types (diamond core and reverse circulation) have been compared for bias.
  • Core recovery, density measurements and downhole surveys have been checked.
  • QA/QC results are documented in the report and in JORC Table 1.

Questions

Why should control samples be blind?

If the laboratory can identify control samples it may, even unintentionally, treat them differently, and their results would no longer represent the quality of routine samples.

What can be done with historical data that has no QA/QC?

Re-assay a share of retained pulps or core, drill twin holes, and measure the bias of the historical data against new data; the outcome affects resource classification.
