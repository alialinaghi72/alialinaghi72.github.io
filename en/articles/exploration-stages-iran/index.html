Stages of mineral exploration in Iran

Exploration is a staged process for reducing uncertainty: each stage should cost less than the next and answer clearly whether the licence deserves further investment. This article reviews the technical stages and the administrative path for exploration in Iran.

Key takeaways

  • Exploration proceeds through reconnaissance, prospecting, general exploration and detailed exploration, with larger scales and higher costs at each stage.
  • Each stage should end with go/no-go criteria agreed in advance.
  • In Iran, exploration starts with registering an area and obtaining an exploration licence from the Ministry of Industry, Mine and Trade, and leads to a discovery certificate once the final exploration report is approved.
  • Data collected with proper QA/QC from the earliest stages can later support a resource estimate that meets international standards.

The four technical stages

  1. Reconnaissance: regional geological maps, previous reports, satellite imagery and airborne geophysics; first field visits and selection of promising areas.
  2. Prospecting: geological mapping and geochemical sampling of stream sediments, soils and rocks to define anomalies; the aim is to shrink the area to a few clear targets.
  3. General exploration: large-scale maps, trenches, ground geophysics and the first drillholes to confirm mineralisation and make a first estimate of size and grade.
  4. Detailed exploration: systematic drilling, sampling with QA/QC, density measurements, metallurgical testing and a resource estimate as the basis of feasibility studies.

Each stage brings larger map scales, denser data and higher cost. The sequence is consistent with international frameworks and with the geological confidence axis of the UNFC classification.

Tools for each stage

In the early stages, remote sensing and geochemistry screen large areas cheaply. Closer to the target, ground geophysics and drilling take over. In detailed exploration, 3D modelling and resource estimation turn the data into a number decisions can rest on.

The administrative path in Iran

In Iran the legal framework for mining is set by the Mining Law and its executive regulations, and licences are issued by the Ministry of Industry, Mine and Trade. The general path is:

  1. Register the requested area in the mining cadastre system and check overlaps with other areas.
  2. Obtain an exploration licence based on an exploration work programme, supervised by a qualified person.
  3. Carry out the work and submit the final exploration report.
  4. Have the report reviewed and approved, and receive a discovery certificate for a mineral with a defined reserve.
  5. Prepare a mining plan and apply for an exploitation (mining) licence.

Administrative details, documents and deadlines can change as regulations are amended; check the latest rules with the official authorities before acting.

Staged decision making

Most exploration spend goes into drilling. A good exploration programme states before each stage which results will lead to continuing and which to stopping — for example a minimum intercept thickness and grade, or anomaly continuity across several profiles. This avoids spending on areas without enough evidence and is transparent for investors.

Common mistakes

  • Drilling before mapping and geochemistry are complete, with no deposit model to target against.
  • No QA/QC in early sampling, which invalidates the data for a later resource estimate.
  • Poor recording of coordinates, downhole surveys and core recovery.
  • No budget for density measurements and metallurgical tests, which resource and reserve classification require.
  • Reporting results in a form that cannot be reconciled with international standards, making investment harder to attract.

Questions

How long does exploring a licence take?

Depending on the size of the area, the commodity and access, from a few months for prospecting to several years for the detailed exploration of a large deposit.

Can historical exploration data be used?

Yes, once validated: check coordinates, assay methods and QA/QC, and re-assay or drill confirmation holes where needed. Validated historical data can cut exploration cost substantially.
