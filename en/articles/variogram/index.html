The variogram explained: calculation, modelling and interpretation

The variogram is the core geostatistical tool for describing the spatial continuity of grade. Almost every decision in a resource estimate — from kriging weights to drill spacing and classification — depends on it.

Key takeaways

  • The variogram is half the average squared difference between samples separated by a distance h.
  • The nugget is the variability at near-zero distance, including sampling and assay error; the sill is the total variance and the range is the distance at which correlation disappears.
  • Experimental variograms must be calculated separately for each geological domain, with enough pairs in every lag.
  • The spherical model is the most common for grades; the Gaussian model is rarely appropriate for grade.
  • The nugget-to-sill ratio and the directional ranges steer the estimation method, block size and drill spacing.

What does a variogram measure?

Two samples a short distance apart usually have more similar grades than two samples hundreds of metres apart. The variogram quantifies this "dissimilarity as a function of distance":

γ(h) = 1 / (2N(h)) · Σ [ z(xᵢ) − z(xᵢ + h) ]²

N(h) is the number of sample pairs separated by the vector h. Because h has both length and direction, variograms can be calculated in different directions to show how continuity differs along strike, down dip and across the thickness of a mineralised zone. Strictly this is the semivariogram, but in practice it is simply called the variogram.

Nugget, sill and range

  • Nugget (C₀): the variogram value at a distance close to zero; a combination of variability at scales smaller than the sample spacing and the errors of sampling, preparation and assay. The name comes from the behaviour of free gold nuggets.
  • Sill (C₀ + C₁): the level at which the variogram flattens; for stationary data it is close to the data variance.
  • Range (a): the distance beyond which samples are no longer meaningfully correlated. Samples within the range of the estimation point receive meaningful kriging weight.

The nugget-to-sill ratio is one of the most important indicators. In the widely used classification of Cambardella et al. (1994), a ratio below 25% indicates strong spatial dependence and above 75% weak dependence; these limits are approximate guides, not hard rules.

Calculating the experimental variogram

Because samples are not exactly equally spaced, pairs are grouped into distance "lags". The main parameters are:

  • Lag length: usually close to the average sample or drillhole spacing in that direction.
  • Lag tolerance: often half the lag, so each pair falls into one lag only.
  • Angular tolerance and bandwidth: how far pairs may deviate from the target direction and still be accepted.
  • Number of pairs: lags with few pairs — as a rule of thumb fewer than 30 — scatter widely and should not drive the model.

A downhole variogram with a lag equal to the composite length gives the best estimate of the nugget, and a variogram map helps find the directions of maximum and minimum continuity. Always calculate on equal-length composites and separately for each geological domain.

Variogram models

The experimental variogram is fitted with an admissible mathematical function so it can be used in kriging. The three basic models:

Spherical: γ(h) = C₀ + C₁ [1.5 (h/a) − 0.5 (h/a)³] for h ≤ a ; C₀ + C₁ for h > a

Exponential: γ(h) = C₀ + C₁ [1 − exp(−3h/a)]

Gaussian: γ(h) = C₀ + C₁ [1 − exp(−3h²/a²)]

  • Spherical: the most common model for grade; nearly linear near the origin, reaching the sill at the range.
  • Exponential: approaches the sill gradually; in these formulas a is the practical range, where about 95% of the sill is reached.
  • Gaussian: very smooth near the origin, better suited to highly continuous variables such as thickness or topographic surfaces than to grade.

Most deposits are fitted better by two or three nested structures — for example a short-range and a long-range structure. To see how each parameter changes the curve, try the interactive variogram lab.

Anisotropy

Grade continuity is rarely the same in every direction; in a vein, continuity along strike and down dip is far greater than across it. In geometric anisotropy the sill is the same in all directions and only the range changes, described by a three-axis ellipsoid. In zonal anisotropy the sill itself changes with direction, which often signals layering or insufficient domaining. The principal directions should agree with the geology: the strike and dip of the zone and the controlling structures.

Interpreting variograms in practice

  • High nugget: common in vein gold; local prediction is difficult, so consider top-cutting, indicator kriging or simulation, and improving the sampling protocol.
  • Short range relative to drill spacing: if holes are further apart than the range, block estimates collapse towards the local mean and classification must be conservative.
  • Proportional effect: in lognormal data the local variance rises with the local mean; pairwise relative or normal-score variograms show the structure more clearly.
  • Continuous rise with no sill: a sign of drift or mixed populations, usually solved by better domaining.
  • Hole effect: a regular oscillation that may reflect repeating structures such as parallel veins.

Variograms and drill-hole spacing

Variogram ranges are a good starting point for drill spacing: the smaller the spacing relative to the range, the more confident the estimate. Turning that principle into a fixed classification number is not sound, though. Better approaches are drill-spacing studies with kriging variance or conditional simulation, which show how accurately quarterly or annual production would be estimated at each spacing. For how this confidence maps onto resource categories, see resources versus reserves.

Questions

What is the difference between the variogram and the covariance?

For stationary data they are linked by γ(h) = C(0) − C(h): the variogram measures dissimilarity and the covariance similarity as a function of distance. The variogram needs a weaker assumption and is more common in mining.

How many samples make a reliable variogram?

There is no fixed number; what matters is the number of pairs per lag and coverage of the main directions. In data-poor domains, use a downhole variogram for the nugget and conservative geological assumptions for the range.
