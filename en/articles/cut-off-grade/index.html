Cut-off grade: what it is and how to calculate it

The cut-off grade is the boundary between ore and waste — the number that decides which part of a deposit is worth mining and processing. This guide works through the formulas with numbers and shows why cut-off grade is never a fixed value.

Key takeaways

  • The break-even cut-off is the grade at which the net revenue from a tonne of ore exactly covers its costs.
  • The marginal cut-off excludes mining cost and applies to material that has to be mined anyway.
  • Price, metallurgical recovery, payability and selling costs affect cut-off as much as operating costs do.
  • Raising the cut-off lowers tonnage and raises average grade; the grade–tonnage curve shows the trade-off.
  • Under JORC, a resource cut-off must be consistent with reasonable prospects for eventual economic extraction, with its assumptions disclosed.

Cut-off grade in plain language

Every block in a deposit model can go to the mill, a low-grade stockpile or the waste dump. The cut-off grade is the lowest grade at which sending a block to a given destination pays. It is therefore not a geological property but an economic decision that depends on metal price, costs, recovery and mine strategy, and it changes whenever any of them changes.

The break-even cut-off formula

In its simplest form, the break-even cut-off is the cost per tonne of ore divided by the net revenue per unit of grade:

Cut-off = (Mining + Processing + G&A, $/t ore) / [ (Price − Selling cost) × Recovery × Payable ]

Costs are in dollars per tonne of ore, price and selling costs (smelting, refining, freight) in dollars per unit of metal, and recovery and payability as fractions. For base metals with grade in percent, multiply the result by 100; for gold, convert the price from dollars per ounce to dollars per gram by dividing by 31.1035.

Worked examples: copper and gold

Copper (hypothetical inputs): price $9,000/t Cu, selling cost $1,000/t Cu, recovery 88%, payable 96.5%, mining $2.5, processing $12 and G&A $2 per tonne of ore.

(9000 − 1000) × 0.88 × 0.965 = 6,793.6 $ per tonne Cu
Break-even = (2.5 + 12 + 2) / 6,793.6 × 100 ≈ 0.24 % Cu
Marginal (excluding mining) = (12 + 2) / 6,793.6 × 100 ≈ 0.21 % Cu

Gold (hypothetical inputs): price $2,000/oz, refining $5/oz, recovery 90%, payable 99.5%, mining $3, processing $18 and G&A $4 per tonne.

(2000 − 5) / 31.1035 × 0.90 × 0.995 ≈ 57.4 $ per gram
Break-even = (3 + 18 + 4) / 57.4 ≈ 0.44 g/t Au
Marginal = (18 + 4) / 57.4 ≈ 0.38 g/t Au

Break-even, marginal and optimum cut-off

  • Break-even cut-off: includes all costs, mining among them; it answers whether a block is worth mining at all.
  • Marginal (in-pit) cut-off: for material that must be removed anyway to reach ore, mining is a sunk cost, so only processing and G&A are compared with revenue.
  • Optimum cut-off (Lane's theory): Kenneth Lane showed that when mining, processing or market capacity is constrained, the cut-off that maximises net present value (NPV) is usually above break-even and declines over the mine life, because the opportunity cost of processing low-grade ore is highest in the early years.

As cut-off rises, tonnage falls and average grade rises, but contained metal — the product of the two — usually falls. The shape of these curves depends on the grade distribution and on block support, which is why a curve read from a small-block kriged model can differ from actual production (see kriging).

Cut-off grade in resource reporting

Reporting codes such as JORC require Mineral Resources to have "reasonable prospects for eventual economic extraction". In practice that means reporting above a realistic cut-off, often within an optimised pit shell at conservative prices, and disclosing the price, cost and recovery assumptions.

Polymetallic deposits use metal equivalents (for example copper equivalent) or net smelter return (NSR). The formula, prices and recovery of each metal must then be disclosed, and equivalents should only include metals with realistic recovery and sale.

Common cut-off grade mistakes

  • Using a high spot price instead of a conservative long-term price.
  • Ignoring smelting and refining charges, freight and payability.
  • Applying cut-off to in-situ grade without allowing for dilution and mining recovery.
  • One cut-off for every domain, even though oxide and sulphide ore differ in recovery or processing cost.
  • Unit errors — price per pound with cost per tonne, or troy ounces mixed up with grams.

Questions

What is the difference between cut-off grade and average grade?

Cut-off grade is the threshold separating ore from waste; average grade is the mean grade of all material above the cut-off and is always higher than the cut-off.

Does the cut-off grade stay fixed over the mine life?

No. It changes with price, costs, recovery and capacity constraints, and the optimal strategy is often a higher cut-off in the early years that declines over time.
